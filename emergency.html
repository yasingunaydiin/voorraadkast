The power is out. What now?

Five rules for the whole time

  • No fire or gas indoors without fresh air. The camping stove, the gas lamp and the fire place give off carbon monoxide, which you can't smell. Fire place: outside only.
  • Candles never go unattended, and never where the cat can knock them over. Use a jar or a holder.
  • Phone is for information, not for fun. Low power mode, screen dim, no scrolling. Power banks are for NL-Alert, calls and messages.
  • Call 112 only for danger to life, fire or injury. A blackout or no water on its own is not an emergency call.
  • Don't spend water you can't replace. Drinking first, cooking second, everything else from the rainwater barrel.

1. No electricity

Most blackouts are over within hours. Plan as if it is a few hours, prepare as if it is days.

First 15 minutes Find out what kind of outage this is

  • Stay calm and stay put. Don't go outside in the dark to look around.
  • Light: use a candle or the phone torch for now.
  • Unplug the expensive things (computer, TV, oven, washing machine). Leave one lamp switched on so you see when power returns. The surge when it comes back can damage equipment.
  • Don't open the fridge or freezer. Closed, a fridge stays cold for about 4 hours and a full freezer for about 24 to 48 hours.
  • Check your phone's battery and switch on low power mode. Note the level.

15 to 30 minutes Get information

  • Look up your grid operator's outage page (Liander, Stedin or Enexis, depending on where you live). Mobile data often still works for a while. Don't phone them, the lines are for emergencies and everyone else will try the same.
  • Listen for NL-Alert. Make sure it is on in your phone settings.
  • Radio: iPhones have no FM radio, so a battery or crank radio is your only news source if mobile data also goes down. Until you have one, rely on NL-Alert and neighbours.
  • Tell one person you're OK with one short message, then stop. Every call and message loads the network.
  • Put a drinking-water buffer in place now. Even if the tap works, fill the two empty barrels from the tap and a few pans. Water often follows the power, and this costs nothing.
This one matters most. Tap water usually keeps flowing at first, but pumping stations and high-rise pumps need power. You have about 20 to 60 minutes of free chance to fill containers. Don't waste it.

30 to 60 minutes Set up for the night

  • Choose one room to live in. It will be easier to keep warm and to light.
  • Gather everything in that room: candles, lighters, the gas lamp, the wool blanket, the phone and power banks, water, something to eat, a pen and paper.
  • Warmth. The central heating boiler needs electricity even when it runs on gas, so it will stop. Wear layers, hat and socks. You have a wool blanket.
  • Cook only if you need to, and only in a ventilated place with a window open. Peanut butter needs no cooking, no water and no fuel.
  • Cat: keep the cat inside, check it has water and food, and keep candles out of its reach.
  • Carbon monoxide: your smoke detector does not detect it, so keep a window open whenever gas or fire is in use.

1 to 6 hours Eat the fridge first

  • Eat from the fridge first (meat, fish, dairy, leftovers), when it passes about 4 hours without power. Then the freezer. Don't refreeze anything that fully thawed.
  • Charge plan: two power banks hold about 100 to 110 Wh, which is 7 to 8 phone charges. Charge the phone only when it drops below about 30 %.
  • Solar panel: put the 60 W panel in a window or outside in daylight. In November expect a fraction of 60 W, but it still adds a bit each day. Charge the power banks with it, not the phone directly.
  • Check on neighbours, especially anyone older or on medical equipment. Offer what you can: a charge, a candle, a hot drink.
  • Go to bed on time. Dark evenings are long. Sleeping saves light, power, food and your nerves.

Day 1 to day 3 Settle into a routine

  • Daily rhythm: check information (NL-Alert, radio) at fixed times, for example morning and evening, instead of all day.
  • Light budget: candles 40 pieces, about 160 to 240 hours if tealights. That is plenty for weeks of evenings if you burn one at a time.
  • Cash: you have €200 in small bills. Card terminals and ATMs go down too. Spend small bills on basics first.
  • Write things down (what you used, water level, gas left) so you can plan ahead instead of guessing.
  • Check the gas for the stove. Weigh or shake the canister. A small one burns roughly an hour in total, so plan on one hot meal a day at most.
  • Go out for a short walk in daylight. It helps mood and sleep, and you learn what's happening in the street.

Week 1 Make it last

  • Eat until you feel full, and no more. Rice and pasta cost gas and water; peanut butter costs neither. Mix them so gas lasts.
  • Use the outdoor fire place for cooking when dry and safe, to save the gas canister for emergencies. Keep distance from walls, fences and anything dry.
  • Find where information is posted. In long outages the municipality sets up information points. Ask neighbours, or watch for notices.
  • Swap and share with neighbours: skills, tools, a hot meal. You have a big peanut butter reserve. Others may have fuel, a car charger or a generator.
  • Rest and read. Books need no power. Keep the days structured to protect your mood.

Week 2 and beyond Think long term

  • Keep an eye on the cat's food. 15 kg lasts months for one cat, but wet food and water matter: keep it drinking.
  • When power returns: wait a few minutes before plugging things back in one by one. Throw out food that stood above 4 °C for more than 2 hours. Then restock and update your Voorraadkast list.

2. Then the water stops too

Start here the moment the tap sputters, runs brown or only dribbles. Without power, pumps stop and sewage pumps may as well.

Do not drink from the tap when it comes back until it is announced as safe, or boil it for at least 1 minute. After an outage the water can be contaminated.

First 15 minutes Catch what's left

  • Fill everything now while there is still any pressure: both empty barrels, pans, kettle, bottles, buckets, the sink. Even a trickle fills a bucket in an hour.

  • Label the containers with a marker or tape: DRINK, CLEAN, DIRTY. Mistakes here make you ill.
  • Other hidden water in the house: a hot water tank or boiler holds roughly 80 to 150 L of drinkable water. Let it cool, then open its drain valve at the bottom. Toilet cisterns (the tank, not the bowl) hold 6 to 9 L. Never drink radiator or central-heating water.

30 to 60 minutes Toilet and hygiene

  • Toilet: pour 2 to 3 L from the rainwater barrel into the bowl to flush. Use it only as needed.
  • If the toilet stops working at all (or you hear gurgling, which means the sewer is backing up): stop flushing. Line the bowl or a bucket with a bin bag, use it, tie it up, and keep it outside in a closed container.
  • Hands: wet wipes and wet toilet paper in place of washing. Wash hands after the toilet and before eating, with a little clean water and soap.
  • Teeth: brush with a little toothpaste and very little water. Spit, don't rinse a lot.
  • Toilet paper (24 rolls): fine for weeks. In a bucket toilet, bag it too.

1 to 6 hours Ration and plan

  • Drink enough, don't ration thirst. Cutting below about 1.5 L a day gets you sick. Cut washing and cooking water first.
  • Cook with less water: rice absorbs it all (about 2 parts water to 1 part rice), so none is wasted. Pasta: use just enough water to cover it, break it up, and keep the cooking water for soup. Less water also means less gas.
  • No-water meals: peanut butter (about 5,900 kcal per kg), jars of peas and carrots, which you can eat from the jar.
  • Cat: a cat needs roughly 200 to 300 ml of water a day, more on dry food. Keep its bowl full and fresh.
  • Check for an announcement (NL-Alert, your water company's page). Municipalities and water companies often set up emergency water points in longer outages.

Day 1 to day 3 Find more water

  • Rain: put out buckets and tarps. Rain from roofs is not drinkable raw, but good for washing and flushing, and with boiling for cooking.
  • Making rainwater safe to drink: strain it through a clean cloth, then bring it to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute and let it cool covered. Purification tablets or a filter make this easier and use no gas. Note that boiling doesn't remove chemicals such as roof coatings or fuel, so it is a last resort.
  • Water points: when announced, go with your barrels or bottles. A full 10 L barrel weighs 10 kg, so bring a trolley or a rucksack.
  • Stay hydrated and watch for signs of dehydration: dark urine, headaches, dizziness. They're early warnings to drink.
  • Wash as little as you can, in a bowl: face, armpits and groin first. Change underwear and socks.
  • Painkillers: you have 2 packs of paracetamol and 1 of ibuprofen. Take only with water and food as directed, and don't take both if you don't need to.

Week 1 Run it like a camp

  • Keep a daily water log: litres in, litres out. Adjust if you're losing more than 2 L a day total.
  • Laundry: don't wash clothes. Air them out and spot clean. Wear a clean layer next to the skin.
  • Waste: keep closed bags outside in a lidded bin so they don't attract rats or smell. Follow your municipality's advice on collection.
  • Watch for illness. Diarrhoea in a no-water situation is dangerous: keep drinking clean water with a pinch of salt and some sugar, and get medical help if it lasts more than 24 hours or you have a fever. Salt helps.
  • Pool your resources with neighbours: one fire, one boiling pot, and shared trips to the water point.

Week 2 and beyond Get water for the long run

  • When the tap returns: run the cold tap for a few minutes until it's clear, flush the toilets and run the hot tap too. Follow any boil-water notice for the exact length. Refill the barrels and bottles straight away.
  • Replace what you used and update the list in Voorraadkast. Throw away rainwater that has been standing for weeks.
